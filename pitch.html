The problem

They can tell.

Not consciously. Not in a way they would ever complain about. But somewhere in the two seconds your ad gets, something reads as generic, and they keep scrolling.

That is the real cost of a generated ad. Not that it looks bad. Most of them look fine. The problem is that fine is indistinguishable from every other business in your category who ran a similar prompt through the same model that week.

The tells

Easy to list once you know them. Your customers do not name them. They just feel them.

  • Hands that do not quite work01
  • Text that is almost a word02
  • The same soft gradient behind everything03
  • A face that belongs to nobody04

The biggest tell is not visual at all. It is that the ad could be for anyone. Swap the logo and nothing breaks. That is the part people feel before they can explain it.

What actually works

A small business does not win on polish.

Big brands will always out-polish you. They have the budget and the retainer and the team. You do not win that fight, and you should not try to.

You win on the specifics only you have. The year you opened. The thing you refuse to change. The regular whose order you have memorised. Generated work cannot reach any of it, because it was trained on everyone else.

Proof

This is what an ad looks like when a person makes it.

A clothing brand putting a portion of every sale into suicide prevention programmes. The campaign had two jobs at once: sell a sixteen ounce hoodie, and say something true about why the drop exists.

Blend those two jobs and you cheapen both. So they were separated. The product frames do product work plainly, the weight of the fabric, the price, the free shipping, callouts pointing at what you are actually buying. The message frames stay quiet and give the reason its own room.

Struggle creates growth, set around a bare tree. A beautiful world, under a concrete underpass. Those are judgement calls about tone, made by someone who understood what the money was for. There is no prompt for that.

And when there is history to work with

A trail riding ranch that has been working since 1942.

Rocking B Ranch runs trail rides in Lake George, New York. Same family, same mountain, eighty years. I built their identity this year: the lockup, two colourways, and the apparel it lives on.

The range could have been a logo on a blank shirt. Instead I went into the family archive and pulled photographs of two of their riders, Lawrence and Willis Bennett, and set each one in a framed panel with the region and the year underneath.

Lawrence Bennett was a real person who rode for this ranch. No model has heard of him. It cannot find him, cannot draw him, and cannot invent the eighty years that make him worth printing on a shirt.

That is what you are actually buying when you hire a designer. Not the polish. The part that is only yours.
